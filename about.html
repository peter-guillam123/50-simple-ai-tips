About this guide

How this was made, and what to distrust

A page for anyone who wants to know where the 50 tips came from, how far each has been checked, and how to change them.

What this is

A practical guide for Guardian staff, written for people who don’t use AI much yet or don’t see the point. It lists 50 small, practical things you can do with the tools the Guardian already provides, mostly Gemini inside Google Workspace. Each tip says where the feature lives, gives three or four steps, and where it helps, a prompt you can copy. Nothing in it writes a story or produces copy for publication.

It reads top to bottom like a long list piece, and it can be cut down. Pick what you are holding (a PDF, a spreadsheet, a video) and the list shrinks to what you can do with it. Filters live in the address, so a trainer can send a desk a link to "the five spreadsheet tips". Every tip has its own anchor.

How it was made

Chris Moran, the Guardian's head of editorial innovation and AI, wrote the brief and chose the 50. The page was built from that brief by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, under his direction. He is not a programmer: he describes what he wants and the model does the typing. This page is part of that arrangement.

The guide is a static page: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no build step. All 50 entries live in one readable data file, js/entries.js, and the page draws everything from it: the list, the filters, the counts. The fixed text (the tool cards, the section introductions, the habits between sections, the sources) lives in js/site.js. The interactive plates are drawn by code, not screenshots, so they stay sharp and date more slowly, and none of them reproduces Google's logos.

The look borrows from two earlier pages: the structure of the AI risk field guide (sticky section nav, numbered plates with a "what to notice" box, a sources list with a status on each link, light and dark mode) and the type and colours of the Guardian deck kit behind the vibecoding workshop.

What to distrust

The steps were drafted from Google's announcements, not clicked through on a Guardian account. Google renames and moves buttons often. Where a label could not be confirmed, the text carries a marker that readers never see. Add ?review to the page address and every marker shows in yellow or red, along with a note on each tip about what still needs checking on a staff account.

Two tips are placeholders. The Guardian's own transcription tool (tip 30) and the live transcription extension (tip 31) need their real names, links and steps. They are marked to do.

The plates are illustrations. Every passage, timestamp, planning application and council plan in them is made up to show the mechanism, and each plate says so. None is from a real document.

The sources were checked in the brief, not by the page. The sources list marks each link as checked in the project brief on 22 and 23 October 2026, or still to add. The page itself has not followed them.

Availability varies. Some features depend on the Workspace edition, admin settings or a rollout that was still under way when this was written. The review view lists the checks that remain.

Diary

What changed and why, newest first, in Chris's voice.

  • 26 October 2026, later

    The page said the same thing twice: a "Searching with AI" section near the top, then a "Pick the right tool" section that covered the same four tools. Now there is one. Part one of the list is called "Searching with AI", opens with the interactive ladder, and each rung links down to its tip. The panel on how reliable AI Overviews are now sits under the AI Overviews tip, where it belongs, rather than under AI Mode.

  • 26 October 2026

    Redrew the AI Overview and AI Mode pictures from real screengrabs rather than memory, and folded in the Guardian’s own Gemini FAQ. That corrected one claim of mine: the privacy promise is that content is not used for training outside the Guardian’s domain and conversations are not reviewed outside it, which is what the panel now says. It also added the thing a reader with no Gemini icon most needs to know: it appears once you’ve done the AI Essentials training and read the acceptable use policy. The data privacy team’s three rules and the links to the policies are in the panel too. Later the same day: a panel after tip 2 on how reliable AI Overviews are, with the two independent tests from the working group deck and their caveats, a Guardian investigation, and no verdict beyond "click through". Also a line wherever a tool takes a picture as well as words, which the first draft forgot.

  • 23 October 2026, evening

    Threw out the 41 drawings and replaced them with ten frames, one per tool surface: a search page, a chat, the three-pane notebook, a mail window, Drive, a spreadsheet, a document, a video player, a Meet call and a phone. Each frame has a few variants, and each draws to the same rules: one canvas, one stroke weight, text shown as grey bars, one yellow highlight for the thing to notice, one red mark for where you are. Every drawing now carries a caption saying what it shows. The old ones were fine in principle and rough in practice; these are quieter and they match each other, which is most of what "refined" means.

  • 23 October 2026, later

    Reworked the top of the page after reading it as a reader would. The eyebrow no longer calls anyone a beginner, and the headings say what the section is rather than hinting at it: "Searching with AI", not "Four places to ask". The tools are now four groups: the Workspace apps you already use, with Gemini built in; general AI tools, including ChatGPT Enterprise for staff on the enterprise list; the Guardian’s own tools, Ask the Guardian, House, Guardian Angles and the two transcription tools; and public tools open to anyone. The "why these" panel now says the thing that matters: Workspace gives you the same privacy you already have in Drive and Gmail, personal and free accounts do not, and anything you would not keep in Drive should not go into an AI tool either. New tools need budget and Tech Council approval. I also went through every tip’s opening line and rewrote the ones that assumed you already knew what a feature was. Each now says what the thing is before what it’s for.

  • 23 October 2026Milestone

    First full build. All 50 entries are in, with steps, prompts and the taxonomy from the brief: nine jobs, 13 tools, nine materials, three levels of effort. The page reads top to bottom, filters by any mix of those, and keeps the filters in the address so a link can carry them. Every tip has an anchor and a click-to-copy prompt.

    Nine interactive plates: the tool ladder at the top, then one each for finding everything on a topic, the citation receipt, document to data table, what changed between versions, jumping to a moment in a video, filling a column with one formula, a skill reused all week, and the morning digest. The other entries get a small drawing, 41 in all, or nothing. I'd rather a tip had no picture than a bad one.

    A review mode (?review) shows the markers where I couldn't confirm a button name, and the availability note on each tip. Readers never see them. The password gate from the deck is built in but off, and hosting is still an open question. The transcription tools are placeholders until I fill in their names.

    Not done: the presenter view for workshops, and the real word count for the Burnham speech in tip 18, which should be generated from the actual speech and date-stamped rather than invented.
